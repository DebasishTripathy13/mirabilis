Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models are cheaper to run than dense models of the same size because they use sparse activation: during inference, only a small subset of the model’s parameters (typically 1–2 out of many “experts”) are activated for each input, rather than computing all parameters as in dense models. This drastically reduces the computational load per forward pass—even though the total number of parameters may be large, the effective computation is comparable to a much smaller dense model. This sparsity enables MoE models to scale to hundreds of billions of parameters without proportionally increasing FLOPs or memory usage during inference.

Additionally, MoE models benefit from hardware-efficient parallelism: since each expert can be assigned to different GPUs or compute units, the workload is naturally distributed across devices. This allows systems to leverage parallel infrastructure more effectively, reducing the time and energy required per inference. In contrast, dense models require all parameters to be loaded and computed on the same device or synchronized across devices, leading to higher memory bandwidth demands and communication overhead. As a result, MoE architectures deliver higher model capacity with lower operational costs, making them more scalable and efficient for large-scale deployments.
